<h2 id="motivation">Behavior has names. Bodies need commands.</h2>

Behavioral neuroscience and embodied AI both describe behavior with discrete vocabularies. Keypoint-MoSeq splits motion into syllables, pose classifiers assign discriminative classes, annotators write ethograms, and planners emit symbols. Over such a vocabulary, planning and annotation become tractable. But none of these symbols can be *executed*: they describe recorded motion, and making a body perform one usually takes a hand-engineered controller or a policy trained, with its own reward, for that symbol alone.

Controllers that do act through a discrete bottleneck learn their own codebook, optimized to reconstruct motion, so any planner, language model or annotation scheme that issues commands has to adopt the controller's vocabulary instead of its own. **Code2Act** inverts this for the 38-actuator biomechanical rat of [MIMIC-MJX](https://mimic-mjx.talmolab.org/). The vocabulary is defined outside the controller and fixed before training; the controller never shapes it. One recurrent controller reads only that symbol stream and the body's proprioception, maps each symbol into the latent space of a frozen, pretrained imitation model, and decodes it to torques. A single training run covers an entire vocabulary, and the vocabulary can be swapped for any other labeling without changing the controller's design.

On this page we walk through what the controller does, with the rollouts behind the paper's figures rendered as video and several of the analyses made interactive.

<h2 id="method">How Code2Act works</h2>

Code2Act is built in three stages, each run as its own process. (1) A pretrained imitation model supplies both a privileged teacher (its frozen encoder) and an inverse-dynamics decoder. (2) An external vocabulary assigns every reference frame a discrete symbol; it can be any labeling, and the controller never changes it. (3) A recurrent controller learns, by reinforcement learning on its own rollouts, to reproduce the motor intention the imitation model would have produced from the full reference, while seeing only the symbols. Stage 3 is the contribution: Stage 1 is the model it decodes through, initialized from the imitator and fine-tuned with the controller, and Stage 2 is its input.

The key property comes from the pretrained latent. The imitation model's intention encodes the change the body must apply to reach the next pose *from its current state*, so no single intention can serve a symbol across every body state. One symbol therefore maps to many intention trajectories, each selected by the body's state at that moment. That is why a held symbol can reach the same motion from very different starting points, as the experiments below show.

<h2 id="vocabularies">Three vocabularies, one controller</h2>

Behavioral neuroscience already maintains many such vocabularies, built independently of any controller. We train the same controller design on three of them (one training run each) that differ in where they come from and how large they are:

- **KPMS**: unsupervised Keypoint-MoSeq syllables, *K* = 100. Usage is heavy-tailed; the 25 most-used syllables cover about 97% of frames.
- **KVAE**: latents of a kinematics VAE on short pose windows, clustered with *K*-means into *K* = 25 codes.
- **MLB**: nine manually labeled behaviors, Walk, Rear and Turn crossed with Left, Straight and Right, plus Immobile. MLB is written by hand rather than fit to the corpus, as a human annotation scheme would supply it. Because the three differ in origin, in size, and in whether they were fit to data at all, any property that holds for all three belongs to Code2Act rather than to a particular vocabulary.

Executed on the body, not every symbol is distinguishable. The nine MLB symbols steer to about five distinct behaviors: there are only about 100 labeled Walk R frames in the corpus, and a rear is the same movement whether it is labeled left or right. Keypoint-MoSeq concentrates its usage on about 7 of its 100 syllables, while the KVAE codebook stays near its full size (an effective 24 of 25 codes). The check therefore discriminates between vocabularies rather than always finding collapse: running a vocabulary on a body shows which of its symbols are distinct actions, which are redundant, and which distinctions the data cannot support.

<h2 id="realism">Symbols steer realistic behavior</h2>

Checking individual symbols is only meaningful if the controller's motion looks like a rat. We quantify realism with the kernel inception distance (KID) between controller rollouts and the expert MIMIC-MJX policy, computed in the feature space of a small VAE trained on expert motion. Each controller is driven by five code streams: its original codes, samples from the empirical transition matrix, two learned autoregressive priors (GPT and Mamba), and uniformly random codes.

<h2 id="dynamics">Inside the controller</h2>

The body and the controller organize the same symbols differently. We compare per-symbol dynamics with dynamical similarity analysis (DSA), once on the body state and once on the GRU state. On the body, symbols split into held postures and locomotion: the three rears group with Immobile, and the walks and turns form the other block. Inside the controller the split moves: Walk L and Walk S separate from everything else, and the turns group with Walk R rather than with the other walks. This mirrors the data: in the corpus a turn is mostly a curving walk, and Walk R is the rarest symbol.

<h2 id="roundtrip">The same symbol, from anywhere</h2>

A symbolic layer needs each symbol to mean the same behavior whenever it is issued, from whatever state the body is in. A symbol that merely replayed a stored trajectory would work from one start pose. Because each symbol is defined outside the controller, re-labeling the motion it produces scores the controller against a criterion it was never trained on. We hold each symbol from 60 start poses and let the vocabulary's own labeler read the motion back, a test we call *label recovery*.

For MLB the commanded behavior is recovered in 69% of instantiations and the exact symbol in 48%, against chance levels of 25% and 11%; KVAE reaches 83% and 80%, and KPMS 51% and 48%. Most of the shortfall is the directional collapse seen above: 8 of the 9 symbols place their most common readout inside the correct behavior. The exception, Turn L, reads as Walk L in 98% of instantiations, which makes it the most consistent symbol in the codebook, executed as a curving walk.

<h2 id="perturbation">Kick it, and it comes back</h2>

Label recovery only varies the start pose. We also perturb the body during execution: after it settles under a held symbol, a single-step velocity impulse of scale *a* hits it, and the controller, still reading only the symbol, has to bring it back.

Five seconds after the impulse, 85% (MLB), 92% (KPMS) and 74% (KVAE) of the induced displacement has decayed on average, with little dependence on impulse size. Across a 6.7-fold range of impulse, MLB's peak displacement grows 7.9-fold but its final displacement only 2.0-fold. The deviation from unperturbed motion grows with the impulse yet stays below the distance between distinct symbols for every behavior at *a* = 0.6. For MLB, rears cross that line at *a* = 1.2 and turns at *a* = 4.0, while walks and immobility never do; a rear sits near the limit of static stability. Recovery is consistent across every tested symbol and vocabulary but depends on posture: rear, the posture nearest the limit of static stability, is the least stable.

The resulting motion is less realistic than corpus-derived sequences but stays far from the random-code level, and the commanded behaviors remain recognizable pair by pair. Sequences with no support in the data still fall within the distribution of rat motion.

<h2 id="discussion">Discussion</h2>

Code2Act executes externally defined behavioral vocabularies on a biomechanical rat without the controller ever shaping the vocabulary. Across three vocabularies of different origin, commanded symbols produce realistic motion, keep recognizable behaviors across diverse initial states, recover from physical perturbation, and compose into sequences the data never contained. For neuro-symbolic embodied agents, this lets a higher-level reasoning system keep its own action vocabulary while its symbols are grounded in physical control. For neuroethology, it lets a behavioral vocabulary be evaluated by executing it rather than only describing it: execution reveals which symbols correspond to physically distinguishable behaviors, and different labeling methods can be compared on the same body.

Several limitations remain. Code2Act needs a pretrained imitation model for the body of interest. Fine behavioral distinctions, such as direction, can be lost when the training data holds too few examples of them, so richer or more balanced data should improve symbol-level fidelity. Longer composed sequences may drive the body outside the training distribution and degrade control; integrating planners or language models with mechanisms that correct execution drift is a natural next step. Finally, symbol-level accuracy is measured with the same behavioral labelers that define and recover each vocabulary.
